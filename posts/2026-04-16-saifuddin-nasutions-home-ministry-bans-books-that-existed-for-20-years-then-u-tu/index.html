Saifuddin Nasution’s Home Ministry Bans Books That Existed for 20 Years — Then U-Turns After Backlash, Proving PH Censors First, Thinks Later#

📌 What Happened#

On April 15, 2026, the Ministry of Home Affairs under Minister Saifuddin Nasution Ismail issued a prohibition order banning two books: Memoir Shamsiah Fakeh: Dari Awas ke Rejimen Ke-10 and Komrad Asi (Rejimen 10): Dalam Denyut Nihilisme Sejarah. The ministry claimed the books contained “communist elements and ideologies” that were “a danger to national security.” A third book — a Malay translation of an academic study on Mao Zedong funded by the PKR-led Selangor state government — was also banned. Shamsiah Fakeh’s memoir had been in circulation for over 20 years, first published in 2004 by Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia. After widespread backlash from civil society groups, international press freedom organisations like Article 19, and even PH’s own MPs — including PKR’s Tan Kar Hing (Gopeng) — Saifuddin announced on April 23 that his ministry was in the process of revoking the ban on all three books.

🔥 Why It Matters#

This is not an isolated incident. Government data show that 26 titles were prohibited in the first four months of 2026 alone — surpassing previous years’ totals. The South China Morning Post, in a May 2 analysis, framed the book bans as evidence that Anwar’s government “has tilted to the right in recent years in a move to win the support of Malay voters.” For a coalition that came to power on reformist credentials and promises to protect civil liberties, the accelerating rate of book bans under PH represents a measurable erosion of the democratic space they swore to defend.

💩 Why It’s Bullshit#

A government that bans a 20-year-old book and then reverses the ban a week later after getting roasted does not have a censorship policy — it has a panic reflex. Saifuddin’s ministry couldn’t explain why a memoir published by a public university in 2004, reprinted multiple times, and studied in academic settings for two decades was suddenly “a danger to national security” in April 2026. The investigation of publisher Gerakbudaya — a respected independent bookstore — for “compliance with legal requirements” was the cherry on top: when you can’t defend the ban intellectually, harass the publisher bureaucratically. And the fact that one of the banned books was funded by PH’s own Selangor state government makes the whole episode a masterclass in incoherence.

📜 Previous Cases#

Malaysia’s book-banning habit predates PH. Under BN, the Printing Presses and Publications Act was wielded routinely to suppress dissent. But PH promised to be different — Anwar himself lectured at Georgetown, Oxford, and St Andrews, positioning himself as an intellectual champion of open discourse. DW reported in October 2025 that more books had been banned under PH in that year alone than in the previous six years combined. The trend has only accelerated in 2026. Human Rights Watch’s 2026 World Report flagged Malaysia’s continued use of “repressive laws to criminalize dissent, censor critical speech, and harass human rights defenders” — under PH’s watch.

🎯 PH Callout#

You ran on reform. You promised Malaysians a government that respects intellectual freedom and doesn’t weaponize colonial-era laws against its own citizens. Instead, you’re banning more books than Muhyiddin and Ismail Sabri combined, investigating independent publishers, and only reversing course when the backlash gets loud enough to embarrass you internationally. The ban-then-U-turn cycle isn’t accountability — it’s proof that your censorship instincts are intact and only your PR team has improved. If you can’t even stand by a book ban for more than a week, maybe stop banning books.
