Anwar’s Term Limit Bill Fails By Two Votes Despite Supermajority — Government’s Answer? ‘We’ll Try Again In June’

2026-03-02
#Politics 

Said by: Anwar Ibrahim, Prime Minister / Azalina Othman Said, Law Minister

Category: Politics

Date: 2026-03-02

📌 What Happened#

On March 2, Malaysia’s Parliament voted down the Constitutional (Amendment) Bill 2026, which would have limited the Prime Minister’s tenure to two terms or 10 years. The bill received 146 votes — just two short of the 148 needed for a two-thirds majority. Anwar’s unity government holds at least 153 seats, meaning the bill should have passed easily. But enough coalition MPs simply did not show up. Law Minister Azalina Othman Said had championed the bill as a way to “bolster institutional integrity.” The following day, the government quietly delayed a vote on the attorney general-public prosecutor separation bill — another flagship reform promise. The government has since pledged to reintroduce the term limit bill in June.

🔥 Why It Matters#

This was supposed to be the easy win — a popular, symbolic reform with broad public support, chosen precisely because it carried minimal political risk. Instead, it became Anwar’s most visible legislative failure, exposing what CNA analysts called “significant coordination gaps and uneven political commitment” within the coalition. The bill’s defeat, combined with the immediate delay of the prosecutor reform bill, signals that PH’s reform agenda is not just stalling — it may be structurally incapable of passing constitutional amendments even with a supermajority, a devastating indictment for a coalition that ran on reformasi.

💩 Why It’s Bullshit#

You hold 153 seats. You need 148. The bill failed because your own MPs couldn’t be bothered to show up. This wasn’t an opposition ambush or a constitutional crisis — it was an attendance problem. And the government’s response? Promise to “try again in June” and insist coalition leaders will make sure MPs actually attend next time. That’s not governance reform; that’s a teacher sending a note home to parents. Meanwhile, SCMP reported Anwar was “mid-laugh, chatting with his deputy” when the results were read out — the image of a prime minister who didn’t take his own flagship reform seriously enough to whip the votes.

📜 Previous Cases#

This echoes a recurring pattern in Malaysian reform politics: grand promises followed by quiet retreats. Mahathir’s PH1 government promised institutional reforms in 2018 and delivered almost none before collapsing in 2020. Anwar himself announced in January 2026 that this year would see a slate of reforms — term limits, prosecutor independence, an ombudsman law. The first bill out of the gate failed. The second was immediately delayed. The pattern is clear: PH treats reform as a media cycle, not a legislative priority.

🎯 PH Callout#

You promised reformasi. You wrote it into your manifesto. You told urban voters — the ones keeping you in power — that you would be different. And on the first real test, you couldn’t even fill the chamber. Don’t blame the opposition. Don’t blame the system. You had the numbers. You had the bill. You had the mandate. What you didn’t have was the discipline, the seriousness, or the political will to deliver. “We’ll try again in June” is not a reform agenda — it’s an admission that you didn’t try hard enough the first time.
