Saifuddin Nasution Bans Three Books for ‘Communist Ideologies’ — Then U-Turns a Week Later After National Backlash

2026-04-23
#Politics 

Said by: Saifuddin Nasution Ismail, Home Minister (PKR)

Category: Politics

Date: 2026-04-23

📌 What Happened#

On April 16, 2026, the Home Ministry under Minister Saifuddin Nasution Ismail issued a prohibition order banning two books — Memoir Shamsiah Fakeh: Dari AWAS ke Regimen ke-10 and Komrad Asi Regimen 10: Dalam Denyut Nihilisme Sejarah — citing communist ideologies and threats to national security. A third book, a Selangor government-funded Malay translation of a text about Mao Zedong, was also banned days later. Independent bookstore and publisher Gerakbudaya, which received the order, announced it would challenge the decision through legal channels. Following fierce backlash from civil society groups, academics, lawmakers, and international press freedom organizations including ARTICLE 19, Saifuddin announced on April 23 that the government would reverse all three bans and that he would personally review future book prohibition decisions.

🔥 Why It Matters#

This is a government that came to power promising to roll back authoritarian controls, including the repressive Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984 — the very law used to issue these bans. Instead of repealing it, PH is actively using it. The ban on Shamsiah Fakeh’s memoir is particularly galling: Shamsiah was a Malay feminist and anti-colonial fighter, and the memoir is reportedly linked to the family of an Anwar ally. Banning historical accounts of the Malayan Communist Party does not protect national security — it shrinks intellectual space and signals that the Madani government is willing to suppress uncomfortable history when politically convenient. The SCMP noted that Anwar’s government has “tilted to the right” to court Malay voters, and these book bans fit squarely within that pattern.

💩 Why It’s Bullshit#

PH promised Malaysians an open, reformist government. They pledged to protect academic freedom, press freedom, and intellectual discourse. Instead, the Home Ministry — under a PH minister — reached for the same colonial-era censorship tools that Barisan Nasional used for decades. The U-turn makes it worse, not better: it shows the ban was issued without proper review, without consulting the publisher, and without any serious assessment of whether these books actually threaten anyone. If the government can ban books on a whim and reverse the ban a week later under public pressure, what does that say about the integrity of the decision-making process? Either the Home Ministry acted recklessly, or it tested the waters and backed off when the backlash hit. Neither explanation is acceptable.

📜 Previous Cases#

Malaysia has a long history of book bans under the Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984 — a colonial-era law BN governments used for decades to suppress works on religion, politics, and history. PH’s first stint in government (2018–2020) under Mahathir also failed to repeal the Act despite explicit manifesto promises. Under the current Madani government, the pattern of democratic backsliding extends beyond book bans: crackdowns on LGBTQ+ communities, increased religious enforcement under Zulkifli Hasan, and the use of police reports and sedition threats against critics all point to a government that has abandoned its reformist brand in favour of political survival.

🎯 PH Callout#

You said you were different. You said Malaysia Madani meant a progressive, open society that valued knowledge, history, and intellectual freedom. You told Malaysians to trust you on institutional reform. Instead, your Home Minister banned books about anti-colonial history, embarrassed the country internationally, and then reversed himself a week later after the backlash made global headlines. If you cannot even manage a book review process without humiliating yourselves, how are Malaysians supposed to trust you with structural reform of the judiciary, the civil service, or the electoral system? Repeal the Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984 — or stop pretending you are any different from the governments you replaced.
