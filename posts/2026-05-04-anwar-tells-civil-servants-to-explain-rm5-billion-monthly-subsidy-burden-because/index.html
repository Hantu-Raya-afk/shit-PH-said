Anwar Tells Civil Servants to ‘Explain’ RM5 Billion Monthly Subsidy Burden — Because the Government Can’t Do the Explaining Itself

2026-05-04
#Economy 

Said by: Anwar Ibrahim, Prime Minister & Finance Minister

Category: Economy

Date: 2026-05-04

📌 What Happened#

Prime Minister Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim, speaking at the Natural Resources and Environmental Sustainability Ministry’s monthly assembly in Putrajaya on May 4, urged civil servants to help “explain” the government’s fiscal strain amid the Middle East energy crisis. He admitted the government is now paying up to RM5 billion a month for RON95 petrol subsidies alone under the Budi95 scheme, conceded that cost of living is a real problem — “Do we have a problem with living cost? Yes, I am not denying it” — and said the government can only provide “partial” help, while insisting fuel prices will stay subsidised.

🔥 Why It Matters#

When a prime minister asks the bureaucracy to do his political communication, it signals a government that has lost control of its own narrative. The RM5 billion monthly subsidy figure is staggering — that’s RM60 billion annualised, dwarfing the original subsidy rationalisation targets PH promised. Rather than presenting a credible fiscal plan or structural reform, Anwar is outsourcing public persuasion to civil servants while citing diplomatic ties with Iran, Russia, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan as proof of energy security. The message is clear: we’re burning through money, we can’t fix it, please help us explain why.

💩 Why It’s Bullshit#

This is the same government that came to power promising subsidy rationalisation, fiscal discipline, and transparency. Three and a half years in, the subsidy bill has ballooned to RM5 billion a month, the PM admits he can only offer “partial” help, and his solution is to ask government employees to do damage control. That’s not governance — that’s outsourcing accountability. Anwar’s admission that “we cannot give total help” is a quiet burial of every campaign promise about protecting the rakyat. And framing Malaysia’s energy dependence on authoritarian regimes like Iran and Russia as a strategic win is the kind of spin that only works if nobody’s paying attention.

📜 Previous Cases#

This follows a well-worn PH pattern: announce a crisis, admit the problem, then ask everyone else to manage expectations. In 2024, the government rolled out targeted diesel subsidy reforms that triggered price spikes and logistical chaos, with ministers blaming “adjustment periods.” Mat Sabu recently told Malaysians to “spend prudently” as food prices rose. PH Youth demanded a “political truce” as the subsidy bill exploded 290%. Every time the fiscal wall gets closer, the government’s playbook is the same — acknowledge pain, deflect blame to external factors, and ask for patience without offering a structural fix.

🎯 PH Callout#

You promised fiscal reform. You promised subsidy rationalisation that would be fair, phased, and transparent. Instead, you’re burning RM5 billion a month and asking civil servants to sell the story for you. “Malaysia Madani” was supposed to mean competent, accountable governance — not “please help us explain why we can’t keep our promises.” If your best pitch to the rakyat is “we can partially help,” then at least have the decency to present a credible long-term plan instead of relying on diplomatic name-drops and bureaucratic PR campaigns.
