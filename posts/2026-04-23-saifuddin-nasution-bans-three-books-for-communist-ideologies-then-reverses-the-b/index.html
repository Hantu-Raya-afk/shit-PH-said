Saifuddin Nasution Bans Three Books for ‘Communist Ideologies’ — Then Reverses the Ban a Week Later After His Own Coalition Calls It Out

2026-04-23
#Politics 

Said by: Saifuddin Nasution Ismail, Home Minister

Category: Politics

Date: 2026-04-23

📌 What Happened#

On April 16, 2026, Malaysia’s Home Ministry — under Minister Saifuddin Nasution Ismail (PKR) — banned two books published by Gerakbudaya: Memoir Shamsiah Fakeh: Dari Awas ke Rejimen Ke-10 and Komrad Asi (Rejimen 10): Dalam Denyut Nihilisme Sejarah, claiming they contained communist ideologies that threatened national security. A third book, Mao Zedong: China dalam Dunia Abad ke-20 — funded by the PKR-led Selangor state government — was also banned. Just seven days later, on April 23, Saifuddin announced the government was in the process of revoking all three bans and introduced a new review process where he would personally vet future bans.

🔥 Why It Matters#

This isn’t a minor bureaucratic hiccup — it’s the Home Minister of a supposedly reformist government using the colonial-era Printing Presses and Publications Act to ban historical books, then backing down within a week when the backlash became politically untenable. One of the banned books was a memoir by the grandmother of an Anwar ally. The publisher, Gerakbudaya, was simultaneously put under investigation. International rights groups including ARTICLE 19 condemned the move. The SCMP’s May 2 analysis frames this as evidence of Anwar’s government tilting rightward to court Malay-Muslim voters — at the cost of the reformist credentials that brought PH to power.

💩 Why It’s Bullshit#

A government that came to power on promises of institutional reform and democratic freedoms is using the same authoritarian playbook as its predecessors — banning books, investigating publishers, and weaponising vague “national security” language against historical scholarship. The reversal doesn’t redeem them; it exposes them. If the ban was justified, why reverse it? If it wasn’t, why impose it at all? The answer is obvious: the ministry acted without thinking, panicked when their own side revolted, and scrambled to save face. Saifuddin’s new “I’ll personally review bans” promise is not a reform — it’s one man replacing a broken process with personal discretion, which is how authoritarian censorship works.

📜 Previous Cases#

Malaysia has a long, ugly history of book bans under the Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984, a law PH once campaigned to reform. Under BN, books by Kassim Ahmad, translations of academic works, and publications touching on race and religion were routinely suppressed. PH promised to end this. Instead, reports indicate a surge in book bans under the current administration, with titles covering LGBTQ themes, Islam, and now historical communism all targeted. The pattern is clear: PH in opposition championed intellectual freedom; PH in government reaches for the same censorship tools when politically convenient.

🎯 PH Callout#

You promised Malaysia “reformasi.” You told civil society you were different. You told publishers and academics they had nothing to fear. And then your Home Minister banned historical books about anti-colonial fighters — including one funded by your own state government — because the word “communist” scared someone in Putrajaya. The reversal isn’t accountability; it’s damage control. If you want to prove you’re not BN 2.0, repeal the Printing Presses and Publications Act. Until then, every time your ministry reaches for the ban stamp, you’re confirming what your critics already know: the reformist mask slips whenever it’s politically convenient.
