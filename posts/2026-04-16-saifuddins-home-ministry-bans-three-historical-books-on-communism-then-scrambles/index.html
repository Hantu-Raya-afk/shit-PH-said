Saifuddin’s Home Ministry Bans Three Historical Books on Communism — Then Scrambles to Reverse After Backlash Exposes PH’s Hollow Reformism#

📌 What Happened#

On April 15–16, 2026, Malaysia’s Home Ministry issued prohibition orders against two books published by Gerakbudaya — Memoir Shamsiah Fakeh: Dari AWAS ke Rejimen ke-10 and Komrad Asi (Rejimen 10): Dalam Denyut Nihilisme Sejarah — citing “communist ideologies” that threaten national security. By April 22, a third book, a Malay translation of an academic text on Mao Zedong, was also banned. Home Minister Datuk Seri Saifuddin Nasution Ismail’s ministry used the Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984 — the same colonial-era censorship law that PH vowed to repeal when in opposition. Civil society groups, PEN Malaysia, international press freedom body Article 19, and even PH-aligned lawmakers condemned the move. After sustained backlash, the ministry announced on April 24 it would “consult publishers before banning books” and began the process of revoking the bans.

🔥 Why It Matters#

This is not a bureaucratic mix-up — it is a pattern. PH came to power in 2018 and again in 2022 on promises of institutional reform, media freedom, and the repeal of repressive laws including the Printing Presses Act. Instead, the law remains on the books and is now being actively wielded against historical scholarship. The SCMP noted on May 2 that Anwar’s government has “tilted to the right in recent years in a move to win the support of Malay voters.” Banning books about Malaysia’s own anti-colonial history — specifically targeting Malay-language translations — signals that PH’s reformist credentials are not just stalled but actively eroding under electoral pressure.

💩 Why It’s Bullshit#

The books in question are not recruitment pamphlets. Shamsiah Fakeh’s memoir has been in circulation for nearly 20 years. The Malayan Communist Party signed a peace accord in 1989 and ceased to exist. Banning academic and historical texts about a defunct movement under the guise of “national security” is intellectually fraudulent. The fact that only Malay-language editions were targeted — while English and Chinese versions remained available — raises uncomfortable questions about who exactly the government thinks needs to be “protected” from history. And the scramble to reverse course after backlash makes it worse: it shows the ministry either didn’t think it through, or hoped nobody would notice.

📜 Previous Cases#

Malaysia has a long history of using the Printing Presses Act to suppress inconvenient narratives. Under BN, books by opposition figures, academics, and journalists were routinely banned. PH’s 2018 manifesto explicitly promised to repeal or reform the Act. In 2019, during PH’s first stint in government, the Act was used to ban a comic book about the 1MDB scandal. Now, in PH’s second term, the same playbook is being dusted off — this time against historians. The publisher SIRD was also placed under investigation, echoing BN-era intimidation tactics against independent media and publishers.

🎯 PH Callout#

You promised Malaysians a new Malaysia — one where ideas are debated, not banned. You told us the Printing Presses Act was a relic of authoritarianism. You are now using it. If you cannot handle a 20-year-old memoir by a Malay woman who fought the British, what exactly is your “reformasi” reforming? The reversal is welcome, but it does not erase the intent. Fix the law or stop pretending you are different from the people you replaced.
